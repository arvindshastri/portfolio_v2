## Overview

Orderly was designed for McMaster University's SFWRENG 4HC3 course in Fall 2024. The project covers the ideation, design process and usability testing of a high-fidelity prototype for a task management app for people with hectic daily schedules.

Unlike a standard to-do list, Orderly focuses on priority-based scheduling and study buddy matching, for first-year university students and busy professionals. The aim was to reduce the stress and overhead of managing tasks, which often leads to missed deadlines and poorly spent time.

## User research

Our research found that people struggle to know which task to pick up at any given moment, and end up spending more time planning work than doing it.

From this, we defined the core needs:

- Change and reprioritize tasks easily, in fewer than five clicks
- Search for study buddies by course and availability
- Label tasks by priority, so high-value work is easy to spot

## Design process

Ideation used brainstorming, scenario analysis and rapid ideation with sticky notes. We generated 20 ideas and chose priority-based scheduling and study buddy matching to build.

## Testing

Early sessions with volunteer participants, fourth-year Software Engineering students, found the interface "aesthetically pleasing", but surfaced some ambiguities:

- The month label didn't obviously switch between day, week and month views
- Item colors could read as priorities instead of task groups
- The deadline markers were ambiguous
- The plus button needed a label to explain what it does

Participants rated intuitiveness 8 out of 10, and suggested smoother transitions between schedule views and more concrete progress tracking. The core task flow made sense, but secondary features needed clearer guidance.

## Final prototype

The high-fidelity prototype answered that feedback. Using the Gestalt principle of proximity, view switching became one clearly labeled dropdown with a down-arrow signifier. A traffic-light model, red, yellow and green for high, medium and low priority, matched how people already think, backed by a new priority legend.
